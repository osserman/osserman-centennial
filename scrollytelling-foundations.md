# Slide 2 — The parallel postulate

## Title

**The parallel postulate and its implications**

## Body

One of these few assumptions was the **parallel postulate**, though it doesn't actually start with parallel lines. It started with any two lines... 

And another line that intersects them. 

If the two interior angles on one side add up to less than 180°, Euclid reasoned the original lines will eventually meet on that side. 

If they add up to 180° exactly, the lines are parallel and will never meet. 

From his postulate, Euclid proved a remarkable chain of consequences. Including that any line crossing two parallel lines creates alternate interior angles that are equal.

And from this, that the inner angles of triangles always add up to 180°.

# Slide 3 - Living on the surface of a sphere

## Title

**Living on the surface of a sphere** 

## Body

On surfaces that aren't flat, this foundational geometry doesn't quite work out. 

Start at the equator of a sphere. 
* Take the shortest path to the north pole by heading due north. 
* Turn right 90° and take the shortest path back to the equator. 
* Turn right 90° again until you arrive back where you started. 

Here you have a spherical equalatoral triangle, whose angles add up to 270°. In fact if you take any triangle along the surface of a sphere, the angles will always add up to more than 180°. 

Furthermore, parallel lines themselves behave different. Start from a line segment of the equator and drew parallel lines at right angles from the equator. They not only intersect, but they intersect twice, once at the north pole and again at the south pole. On a sphere, all lines that would be parallel on a flat surface intersect exactly twice. 

These characteristic of spherical geometry where known, living alongside Euclid's geometry for centuries. But the mathematics of it took over a thousand years to develop fully. # Slide 4 — Gauss' Survey on our Curved Planet

## Title

**Gauss' Survey on our Curved Planet**

## Body

Advances in math require immense imagination and ingenuity, but they are not imaculately born in the head of mathematicians. They can be seeded by forebearers and contemporaries, by keen observation, and sometimes by trying to find a more elegant way through a practical problem. 

One such problem arrived in 1818, when Carl Friedrich Gauss, a famed mathematician, physicist, and astronomer, was given the task of surveying the relatively new Kingdom of Hanover. While no new math was needed to complete this task, he took the opportunity to make a look deeper, in the process making a great advance in the study of curved surfaces. 

For centuries, triangles had been tools for surveying, mapping the world. But he developed the math to understand the relationship between distances and angles and the curves of the surfaces they are measured on. It enabled people for the first time to deduce the precise qualities of the surface's curvature from taking measurements on its surface. 

While this could be used for to confirm theories about how the earth deviates from a sphere, buldged at the center, flattened at the poles, it could also be used to explore and explain much more theoretical surfaces.
